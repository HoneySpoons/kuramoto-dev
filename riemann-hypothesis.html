The Riemann Hypothesis, from the ground up

1. Some numbers can't be split

Take a pile of pebbles. Try to lay them out as a full rectangle, with more than one row.

Most piles can do it. Some can't: however you stack them, there are always some left over. A number like that is called a prime.

pebbles
The one ideaA prime is a number you can't make by multiplying two smaller numbers. 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13 …

2. Every number is built from primes

Keep splitting any number and you always end at primes. They are the bricks. Every other number is a wall built from them, and there is only one way to build each wall.

number
The one ideaPrimes are the atoms of counting. That is why people care where they are.

3. They never run out, but they thin out

Here are a hundred numbers in a row. The lit ones are prime. Move far up the number line and look again.

start at
The one ideaThe further you go, the rarer primes get. They never stop.

4. Nobody can say where the next one is

Each bar is the distance from one prime to the next. Look for a pattern.

There isn't one. Sometimes two primes sit side by side. Sometimes there is a long empty stretch. No formula gives the next prime.

The one ideaUp close, primes look random.

5. But from far away, they follow a curve

Stop asking where the primes are. Ask how many there are up to some number. That count is a staircase: one step up at each prime.

In the 1790s Gauss, as a teenager, found a smooth curve that predicts the count.

The one ideaRandom up close, orderly from a distance. Like rain: you can't say where the next drop lands, but you can say how wet the pavement gets.

6. The question: how big is the miss?

Gauss's curve is never exactly right. This picture shows only the miss, measured from Gauss's curve: the prediction minus the real count.

The shaded band is roughly the square root of the number you've counted to. Count to a million and the band reaches about a thousand either side.

The Riemann Hypothesis, in plain wordsThe miss stays inside that band. Forever. That is the whole claim. Everything after this screen is about why it is hard to prove.
(The exact band is the square root times a small, slowly growing factor. The square root is the heart of it.)

7. Why the square root?

Flip a fair coin many times. You expect half heads. You won't get exactly half. How far off will you be?

About the square root of the number of flips. Flip 10,000 times and you miss by around 50 or 100, not by 2,000.

heads minus tails, as you flipthe square-root band

A square-root miss is the signature of a fair process: no bias, no hidden lean.

The one ideaThe hypothesis says the primes are as fair as coin flips. Nothing is secretly pushing them to bunch up or spread out.

8. Coins are fair by design. Primes are not random at all.

Coins drift from an even split. Flip a million times and you expect 500,000 heads. You will typically be off by about 500. The square root of a million is 1,000.

Primes drift from what Gauss's curve predicts. Up to a million the curve says 78,628. The real count is 78,498. Off by 130. The same square root, 1,000.

how far off, as you gothe square-root band, the same in both
Coins: off from an even split
Primes: off from Gauss's curve

With coins we know exactly why. Each flip is independent, and the square-root drift follows from that in a few lines. It has been a theorem for about 300 years.

With primes nothing is flipped. Whether a number is prime was always settled, and each prime constrains the rest: once 3 is prime, every third number after it is out.

The one ideaFor coins, fairness is where you start. For primes, fairness is the thing that had to be proved.

9. A detour: any shape is a stack of waves

Picture an arm spinning at a steady rate, like a pendulum that goes all the way round. Watch only the height of its tip. It rises and falls in the smoothest wave there is. That is one pure tone.

Now bolt a shorter, faster arm onto the end of the first. Then another onto that. The height of the last tip is all those waves added together.

spinning arms

The grey shape is a square wave: flat, a sudden drop, flat, a sudden jump. Nothing about it is smooth. Add arms and the tip learns to draw it anyway. The long slow arm gets the rough shape. Each shorter, faster arm fixes a finer detail, and the sharp corners need the fastest ones.

Working out which arms a shape needs, how long and how fast, is the Fourier transform. Track the tip's sideways position as well as its height and the same arms will draw any outline on a page. A synth adding pure tones is doing the same sum by ear.

The one ideaAny shape, even one with sharp steps, is a list of spinning arms: a speed and a length for each. Find the list and you have the shape.

10. The prime staircase is a stack of waves too

The prime staircase is a shape with sharp steps, so it has a list of arms too. In 1859 Riemann found it. Add them one at a time and watch the smooth line learn where the primes are.

waves added

The staircase here is a close cousin of the one on screen 5: the steps have different heights so the waves fit cleanly.

The one ideaThe primes are a chord. Each wave in it is called a zero. Know all the zeros and you know all the primes.

11. Each wave has a pitch and a volume

Every wave in the prime chord has two settings.

Pitch: how fast it wiggles. Every wave has a different pitch. Nobody argues about these.

Volume: how fast it gets louder as you count higher. This is a dial that could sit anywhere from 0 to 1.

volume dial
The one ideaA dial at ½ means the wave grows like the square root. That is the band from screen 6.

12. The hypothesis: every dial is at exactly ½

Below are the first ten waves as faders on a mixing desk. All ten sit at ½, and the miss stays in its band.

Now push the first fader up. This is make-believe: it shows what would happen if even one wave were louder.

the miss, in units of the square rootthe band
first fader
The one ideaOne loud wave and the miss breaks out of the band. So "the miss stays in the band" and "every fader is at ½" are the same statement. The second is the form mathematicians use.

13. Why it stood for 167 years

There are infinitely many waves. Computers checked the first ten trillion faders. Every one sits at exactly ½.

That is evidence. It is not proof. Proof means a reason that covers all of them at once, including the ones no computer will ever reach.

Nobody could find that reason. It became the most famous open problem in mathematics, with a million-dollar prize on it.

The one ideaChecking a trillion cases is measurement. A proof is a reason why no case can ever fail.

14. What happened on 20 November 2026

A mathematician, Levent Alpöge, working with the AI model Claude, published a proof. Not on paper. It is written in Lean, a language built so a computer can check every step.

Think of a shop drawing set where every dimension is derived from the one before it, and a machine re-derives every single one. This set has about 17 million lines.

15. One piece of the "why" that can be shown

The proof says it grew from an older trick. Here is that trick, on its own.

A curve with a limited number of bends can only cross a line so many times. If you force it to linger at each crossing, flattening against the line, each crossing costs more bends. So fewer crossings fit.

how hard it lingers at each crossing

In 1969 Sergei Stepanov used this to give a new, simpler proof of a small-scale cousin of the hypothesis: build something that must linger at every exception, and show there is no room for exceptions. The author's note says the new proof finds a way to make things "linger" in the world of whole numbers, where that had been out of reach.

Where the simple story endsHow it does that is the part nobody can explain simply yet. That work starts now.

The Riemann Hypothesis

Written 21 November 2026 · one day after the announcement

The Riemann Hypothesis: the question, and what was just proved

Seven pictures. The first five are the question. The last two are the idea behind the answer, as far as anyone has explained it.

Where things stand. On 20 November 2026 Levent Alpöge released a proof, found with the AI model Claude, written entirely in the proof language Lean. Independent checking programs accept it, and the Lean and Mathlib communities have certified it. No person has yet read and understood the whole argument. Pictures 1 to 6 below are settled mathematics. Picture 7 is a map of the author's short note, and is marked as such.

1 · The question starts here

Primes thin out. How evenly?

Count the primes up to x and you get a staircase: it rises by one at every prime. There is no formula for where the next step comes. But a smooth curve, written down by Gauss, follows the staircase closely.

primes up to x (the staircase)Gauss's smooth estimate, Li(x)

Notice: slide the range up. The staircase and the curve stay close, and the gap between them stays small compared with x. The Riemann Hypothesis is a precise statement about how small that gap stays, forever.

2 · The bridge

One function that knows every prime

Euler found that adding over all whole numbers gives the same answer as multiplying over only the primes. This is the zeta function, and it is why a question about primes becomes a question about one function.

ζ(s)  =  1 + 1/2s + 1/3s + 1/4s + …  =  1/(1 − 2−s) · 1/(1 − 3−s) · 1/(1 − 5−s) · 1/(1 − 7−s) · …
sum over whole numbers up to Nproduct over primes up to Nπ²/6
shown at s = 2

Notice: two different recipes, one built from every number and one from primes alone, close in on the same value. Everything zeta does is therefore something the primes do.

3 · The statement

Where is the function zero?

Riemann let s be a complex number, s = σ + it, a point on a plane. In the strip where σ is between 0 and 1, zeta has infinitely many zeros. Each dot in the top band is one of them. The strip is drawn on its side here, so the famous "critical line" σ = ½ runs left to right through the middle. The curve underneath shows the size of zeta along the line you choose with the slider.

size of ζ(σ + it) along your linezeros of zeta

Notice: move σ anywhere except ½ and the curve dips but never touches zero. Set it to exactly ½ and it touches zero again and again. The Riemann Hypothesis: every zero in the strip sits on the line σ = ½. Computers had checked trillions of them. None had ever been found off the line, and nobody could prove none ever would be.

4 · Why the zeros matter

Each zero is one note in the music of the primes

Riemann's formula rebuilds the prime staircase out of waves, one wave for each zero. Here the staircase is the weighted count ψ(x), which steps up by log p at every prime power. Add the zeros one at a time.

the true prime staircase ψ(x)rebuilt from the zeros

Notice: with no zeros you get a straight line, the average. With a hundred, the line has learned where every prime in view is. The zeros are not near the primes or like the primes. They are the primes, written as frequencies.

5 · What is at stake

On the line, every note is as quiet as it can be

A zero at σ + it gives a wave whose loudness grows like xσ. Zeros come in mirrored pairs across the line, so moving one to the right of ½ makes a louder wave. On the line, every wave grows like √x, the slowest growth the mirror allows. This is a thought experiment: drag the first zero off the line and watch the error in the prime count.

error ÷ √x with every zero on the linewith the first zero moved off (not real)

Notice: on the line the error, measured against √x, stays small. With every zero counted it can still creep up, but no faster than (log x)². Off the line it grows like a power of x. So the hypothesis says the primes are as evenly spread as they could possibly be. A great many theorems were proved "assuming the Riemann Hypothesis". This is what they were assuming. (Drawn from the first 20 zeros.)

6 · The version that was already proved

In a smaller world, this has been a theorem for ninety years

Replace the whole numbers with clock arithmetic modulo a prime p, and replace "count the primes" with "count the solutions of a curve". The same question has an exact answer there. For a curve y² = x³ + ax + b, the count of solutions differs from p + 1 by at most 2√p. That bound is that world's Riemann Hypothesis. Hasse proved it for these curves in the 1930s and Weil for all curves in the 1940s. Below, every such curve for your p is counted.

number of curves with each errorthe bound ±2√p

Notice: pick any p. Not one curve lands outside the dashed lines. In 1969 Sergei Stepanov found a way to prove this bound with nothing but polynomials. The new proof calls itself "a descendant of Stepanov's".

7 · The idea behind the answer

Stepanov's trick: make it vanish many times

A polynomial of degree D can be zero at most D times, counting repeats. Stepanov built a polynomial of modest degree that is forced to vanish, not once, but many times over at every solution of the curve. Then the solutions cannot be numerous: there is not enough degree to pay for them.

Notice: raise m. The curve flattens against the axis at each root, and fewer roots fit. More vanishing per point means fewer points allowed. That is the whole trick. (Heights are rescaled between roots so the shape is visible.)

What the new proof says it does

For a polynomial, "vanishes m times" means its first m − 1 derivatives are zero too. Whole numbers have no derivative, which is why Stepanov's method stayed in the smaller world for fifty years. The note's claim is that a different kind of object supplies the missing repeats.

How anyone can be sure

Checked by machine, not yet read by people

This is the exact sentence that was proved. It was written into the Mathlib library by other people, before this proof existed:

def RiemannHypothesis : Prop :=
  ∀ (s : ℂ) (_ : riemannZeta s = 0) (_ : ¬∃ n : ℕ, s = -2 * (n + 1)) (_ : s ≠ 1),
    s.re = 1 / 2

In words: for every complex number s where zeta is zero, leaving aside the known zeros at −2, −4, −6, … , the real part of s is ½. Picture 3, in one line.

The statementMathlib's own definition, vetted earlier by number theorists. The author did not write it.

A proof checker cannot be persuaded, impressed or hurried. It can only confirm that each line follows from the last. What it cannot tell you is why. For the first time on a problem of this size, the checking finished before the understanding began.
